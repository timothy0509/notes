# Competition and Market Structure

## What is a Market?

A **market** is any arrangement that brings buyers and sellers into contact to exchange goods or services. Markets can be physical (e.g. a wet market) or virtual (e.g. an online platform).

---

## Classifying Market Structures

Market structures are classified along a spectrum from **perfect competition** to **pure monopoly**, with **monopolistic competition** and **oligopoly** in between.

> [!info] The key question: how much **market power** does a firm have? Market power is the ability to set price above marginal cost.

---

## General Features of Market Structures

| Feature | Perfect Competition | Monopolistic Competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of sellers** | Very many | Many | Few | One |
| **Number of buyers** | Very many | Many | Many | Many |
| **Nature of product** | Homogeneous | Differentiated | Homogeneous or differentiated | Unique (no close substitutes) |
| **Ease of entry** | Very easy | Fairly easy | Difficult | Blocked |
| **Availability of information** | Perfect information | Moderate | Moderate / imperfect | Imperfect |
| **Price taker or searcher?** | **Price taker** (firm has no control over price) | **Price searcher** (some control over price) | **Price searcher** (strategic interdependence) | **Price searcher** (full control) |
| **Long-run profit** | Zero (normal profit) | Zero (normal profit) | Positive possible (barriers to entry) | Positive (barriers to entry) |
| **Examples** | Agricultural commodities, stock market | Restaurants, hair salons, clothing brands | Airlines, telecom, banks, supermarkets | Water company, MTR, patented drugs |

---

## Perfect Competition

### Conditions
1. Very many buyers and sellers (all are **price takers**)
2. Homogeneous product
3. Free entry and exit
4. Perfect information

### Implications
- Firm faces a **perfectly elastic demand curve** at the market price
- $P = MR = AR$
- Profit-maximising output: $P = MC$
- In the **short run**: the firm can earn supernormal profit or incur losses
- In the **long run**: entry/exit drives profit to zero — $P = MC = AC$ (productive and allocative efficiency)

> [!important] Perfect competition achieves **both** productive efficiency (producing at minimum AC) and allocative efficiency ($P = MC$).

---

## Monopolistic Competition

### Conditions
1. Many sellers
2. **Differentiated product** (real or perceived differences)
3. Relatively easy entry and exit
4. Some market power

### Implications
- Downward-sloping demand curve (firm is a price searcher)
- Profit-maximising output: $MR = MC$
- **Short run**: supernormal profit possible
- **Long run**: entry of competitors erodes profit until $P = AC$ (but $P > MC$, so **allocative inefficiency** persists)

> [!note] Monopolistic competition involves **non-price competition** — advertising, branding, product quality — because firms cannot compete on price alone.

---

## Oligopoly

### Conditions
1. Few sellers — each firm's actions affect rivals
2. **Strategic interdependence** — firms must anticipate rivals' reactions
3. High barriers to entry
4. Products can be homogeneous or differentiated

### Key Features
- **Kinked demand curve** model: explains price rigidity
- **Collusion** (tacit or overt) — firms cooperate to restrict output and raise price
- **Game theory** — the Prisoner's Dilemma shows why collusion is unstable
- **Non-price competition** is common (advertising, R&D, product differentiation)

### Barriers to Entry
- Economies of scale
- High capital requirements
- Patents / legal restrictions
- Brand loyalty / advertising
- Control over key inputs

> [!warning] Oligopoly is difficult to model with a single theory. Different models (Cournot, Bertrand, Stackelberg, kinked demand) explain different aspects.

---

## Monopoly

### Conditions
1. **Single seller** of a product with **no close substitutes**
2. Very high barriers to entry (blocked entry)

### Sources of Monopoly Power
| Source | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Natural monopoly** | One firm can supply the entire market at lower cost than multiple firms (due to massive economies of scale) — e.g. water supply, electricity grid |
| **High set-up cost** | Large initial investment deters entry — e.g. railways, airports |
| **Legal restrictions** | Patents, copyrights, licences, government franchises |
| **Public ownership** | Government reserves the right to produce (e.g. postal service, public utilities) |
| **Control over key inputs** | Firm owns the entire supply of a critical resource |
| **Network effects** | Value of the product increases with more users, creating a self-reinforcing monopoly |

### Implications
- Downward-sloping market demand = firm's demand
- $MR < P$ (since to sell more, price must fall on all units)
- Profit-maximising output: $MR = MC$, then price is read from the demand curve
- Charging $P > MC$ creates a **deadweight loss** — allocative inefficiency
- Monopolist can earn **persistent supernormal profit** due to barriers to entry

> [!example] The MTR in Hong Kong is a legal monopoly (government franchise). It can sustain supernormal profit because entry is blocked by law.

---

## Comparison of Key Outcomes

| Outcome | Perfect Competition | Monopolistic Competition | Oligopoly | Monopoly |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $P$ vs $MC$ | $P = MC$ | $P > MC$ | $P > MC$ (typically) | $P > MC$ |
| Allocative efficiency | Yes | No | No | No |
| Productive efficiency ($P = \min AC$) | Yes (LR) | No (excess capacity) | Not guaranteed | No |
| Supernormal profit (LR) | Zero | Zero | Possible | Yes |
| Innovation incentive | Low | Medium | High (R&D) | High (but may be complacent) |

> [!note] The HKDSE exam often asks you to identify a market structure from a case study. Key clues: number of firms, type of product, and ease of entry.
