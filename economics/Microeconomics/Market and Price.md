# Market and Price

## Demand

### Law of Demand

> [!important] The **Law of Demand** states: as price falls, quantity demanded rises (*ceteris paribus*). Price and quantity demanded are **negatively related**.

This holds because of the:
- **Income effect** — a lower price increases real purchasing power
- **Substitution effect** — a lower price makes the good cheaper relative to substitutes

### Individual Demand vs Market Demand

**Market demand** is the **horizontal summation** of all individual demand curves.

> [!example] If at $P=10$, person A demands 5 units and person B demands 3 units, market demand at $P=10$ is $5+3=8$ units.

### Factors Affecting Demand

| Factor | Effect on Demand |
|---|---|
| **Income** | Normal good: $\uparrow$ income → $\uparrow$ demand; Inferior good: $\uparrow$ income → $\downarrow$ demand |
| **Price of related goods** | Substitutes: $\uparrow P_{sub}$ → $\uparrow D$; Complements: $\uparrow P_{comp}$ → $\downarrow D$ |
| **Tastes / Preferences** | Favorable change → $\uparrow D$ |
| **Population** | More consumers → $\uparrow D$ |
| **Expectations** | Expected future $\uparrow P$ → $\uparrow D$ now |

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Substitutes** | Used in place of each other (e.g. Coke vs Pepsi) |
| **Complements** | Used together (e.g. cars and petrol) |
| **Superior (normal) good** | Demand rises as income rises |
| **Inferior good** | Demand falls as income rises (e.g. instant noodles) |

### Change in Quantity Demanded vs Change in Demand

- **Change in quantity demanded**: movement *along* the same demand curve — caused by a change in the good's **own price**.
- **Change in demand**: *shift* of the entire curve — caused by a change in **non-price factors**.

> [!warning] These two are **not** interchangeable. A price change does NOT "shift demand".

## Supply

### Law of Supply

> [!important] The **Law of Supply** states: as price rises, quantity supplied rises (*ceteris paribus*). Price and quantity supplied are **positively related**.

Higher prices increase the incentive to produce and cover higher marginal costs.

### Factors Affecting Supply

| Factor | Effect on Supply |
|---|---|
| **Cost of inputs** | $\uparrow$ costs → $\downarrow$ supply |
| **Technology** | Better technology → $\uparrow$ supply |
| **Number of sellers** | More firms → $\uparrow$ supply |
| **Expectations** | Expected future $\uparrow P$ → $\downarrow$ supply now |
| **Natural conditions** | Good weather → $\uparrow$ supply (agriculture) |

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Joint supply** | Two goods produced together (e.g. beef and leather) — a rise in demand for one $\uparrow$ supply of the other |
| **Competitive supply** | Two goods competing for same resources (e.g. wheat and corn) — a rise in demand for one $\downarrow$ supply of the other |

### Market Supply

**Market supply** = horizontal summation of individual supply curves.

---

## Market Equilibrium

$$ Q_d = Q_s $$

At the **equilibrium price**, quantity demanded = quantity supplied. There is no shortage or surplus.

### Effects of Changes in Demand/Supply

- **Demand $\uparrow$** → $P\uparrow$, $Q\uparrow$
- **Demand $\downarrow$** → $P\downarrow$, $Q\downarrow$
- **Supply $\uparrow$** → $P\downarrow$, $Q\uparrow$
- **Supply $\downarrow$** → $P\uparrow$, $Q\downarrow$

> [!note] When both curves shift, the direction of change in one variable is ambiguous. It depends on the **relative magnitude** of shifts.

## Consumer and Producer Surplus

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Consumer surplus (CS)** | Difference between **willingness to pay** (marginal benefit) and the **price paid** |
| **Producer surplus (PS)** | Difference between the **price received** and the **minimum supply-price** (marginal cost) |
| **Total social surplus** | $CS + PS$ — maximised at competitive equilibrium |

> [!info] At equilibrium, $MB = MC$, so total surplus is maximised. This is the condition for **allocative efficiency**.

---

## Functions of Prices

| Function | Description |
|---|---|
| **Rationing function** | Prices allocate scarce goods to those willing to pay most |
| **Allocative function** | Prices signal where resources should move (profitable industries attract resources) |
| **Incentive function** | Prices motivate producers to supply more when demand is high |

## Elasticity

### Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

$$ PED = \frac{\% \Delta Q_d}{\% \Delta P} $$

**Arc elasticity** (midpoint formula):

$$ PED = \frac{Q_2 - Q_1}{(Q_1 + Q_2)/2} \div \frac{P_2 - P_1}{(P_1 + P_2)/2} $$

| PED Value | Type | Total Revenue Effect |
|---|---|---|
| $\|PED\| > 1$ | Elastic | $P\uparrow \to TR\downarrow$ |
| $\|PED\| < 1$ | Inelastic | $P\uparrow \to TR\uparrow$ |
| $\|PED\| = 1$ | Unit elastic | $P$ change → $TR$ unchanged |

> [!tip] Revenue is maximised when $PED = 1$ (unit elastic).

### Factors Affecting PED

- Availability of close substitutes (more substitutes → more elastic)
- Necessity vs luxury (necessities are inelastic)
- Proportion of income (higher proportion → more elastic)
- Time horizon (longer time → more elastic)
- Definition of the market (narrow definition → more elastic)

### Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

$$ PES = \frac{\% \Delta Q_s}{\% \Delta P} $$

Factors affecting PES:
- Time horizon (longer → more elastic)
- Availability of spare capacity
- Ease of storage
- Complexity of production

## Market Intervention

### Price Ceiling (Maximum Price)

- Set **below** equilibrium → **shortage**
- Non-price rationing: queues, black markets, lottery
- Deadweight loss (DWL) arises

> [!example] Rent control is a classic price ceiling.

### Price Floor (Minimum Price)

- Set **above** equilibrium → **surplus**
- Government must buy the excess (e.g. agricultural price supports)
- DWL arises

### Quota

- A **physical limit** on output
- Creates a **kinked supply curve** (vertical at the quota quantity)
- Raises price, reduces quantity, transfers surplus from consumers to producers

### Unit Tax

- Shifts supply curve **upward** by the tax amount
- Tax burden shared between consumers and producers depending on elasticities:
  - More inelastic side bears **more** of the tax
- Creates **DWL** (tax wedge between $MB$ and $MC$)

### Unit Subsidy

- Shifts supply curve **downward** by the subsidy amount
- Subsidy benefit shared depending on elasticities
- Also creates **DWL** (subsidy leads to over-production relative to efficient level)

> [!warning] Both taxes and subsidies cause **deadweight loss** because they push the market away from the efficient equilibrium where $MB = MC$.
