# Basic Economic Concepts

## Economics as a Social Science

Economics studies **human behaviour** — how individuals and societies decide on the use of **scarce resources** to satisfy unlimited wants. It is a social science because it analyses the choices people make under **constrained maximisation** (maximising utility/profit subject to resource constraints).

> [!info] Economics does not study money alone; it studies **choice** under scarcity.

---

## Scarcity, Choice and Opportunity Cost

**Scarcity** is the fundamental economic problem: **unlimited wants** but **limited resources**. Because resources are scarce, people must **choose** — and every choice involves a trade-off.

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Free good** | Unlimited supply at zero price (e.g. sunlight, air) |
| **Economic good** | Scarce relative to demand; commands a positive price |
| **Opportunity cost** | The **highest-valued option forgone** when a choice is made |
| **Explicit cost** | Out-of-pocket, monetary payment |
| **Implicit cost** | Foregone income / non-monetary cost |
| **Sunk cost** | Already incurred and **irrecoverable**; irrelevant to future decisions |

> [!warning] Common mistake: opportunity cost is NOT all options forgone — only the **highest-valued** alternative.

**Interest** is the cost of having resources available **earlier** rather than later. It reflects time preference.

> [!tip] Decision rule: ignore sunk costs. Only forward-looking costs and benefits matter.

---

## Three Basic Economic Problems

Every economy must answer:

1. **What to produce** — which goods/services, and in what quantities?
2. **How to produce** — which combination of factors / technology?
3. **For whom to produce** — how is output distributed?

---

## How Society Tackles the Three Problems

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| **Custom / Tradition** | Habits, religion, cultural norms (e.g. traditional economies) |
| **Government decisions** | Central planning / command economy (e.g. North Korea) |
| **Market mechanism** | Prices coordinate decentralised decisions via demand and supply |

> [!example] In a market economy, the price system answers all three questions automatically: prices signal shortages/surpluses, guide resource allocation, and determine who can afford what.

---

## Private Property Rights

**Private property rights** are the exclusive rights to:

- **Use** the resource
- **Receive income** from it
- **Transfer** it (sell/gift/bequeath)

These rights are essential for a market economy. Without them, there is no incentive to invest, trade, or conserve.

> [!important] Well-defined property rights reduce transaction costs and make exchange possible.

---

## Specialisation and Exchange

**Specialisation** occurs when individuals/firms/regions focus on producing a limited range of goods. **Exchange** (trade) must exist for specialisation to be viable — producers trade their surplus for other goods they need.

Benefits of specialisation:
- Higher productivity
- Economies of scale
- Efficient use of comparative advantage

## Circular Flow of Economic Activities

```
         ┌──────────────────────────────┐
         │       Factor payments        │
         │  (wages, rent, interest,     │
         │   profit)                    │
         │                              ▼
    ┌────────┐                 ┌────────────┐
    │HOUSEHOLDS│────labour────▶│   FIRMS    │
    │(owners  │◀───goods───── │ (producers)│
    │of       │                 │            │
    │factors) │────spending───▶│            │
    └────────┘                 └────────────┘
         ▲                              │
         │       Goods & services       │
         └──────────────────────────────┘
```

- **Households** own factors of production; they supply labour and receive income.
- **Firms** produce goods/services; they pay factor incomes.
- **Expenditure** by households = **income** of firms = **value of output**.

> [!note] The three approaches to measuring GDP — output, income, expenditure — correspond to the circular flow.

## Positive and Normative Statements

| Type | Definition | Can be tested? |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive** | Statement about **what is** — factual, objective | Yes — refutable by evidence |
| **Normative** | Statement about **what ought to be** — value judgment | No — depends on ethical views |

> [!example] - Positive: "A rise in the minimum wage reduces employment among low-skilled workers."  
> - Normative: "The government should raise the minimum wage to reduce poverty."

> [!tip] If a statement contains "should", "ought", "better", or "fair", it is almost certainly **normative**.
