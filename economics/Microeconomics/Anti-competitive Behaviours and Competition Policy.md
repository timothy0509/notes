# Anti-competitive Behaviours and Competition Policy

## Major Forms of Anti-competitive Practices

### Horizontal Agreements (Between Competitors)

Agreements between firms at the **same level** of the supply chain that restrict competition:

| Practice | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Price fixing** | Competitors agree on a uniform price rather than competing | Two supermarket chains agree to sell milk at the same price |
| **Output restriction** | Firms agree to limit production to raise prices | OPEC quotas on oil production |
| **Market sharing** | Firms divide territories or customers among themselves | Firm A serves District A; Firm B serves District B |
| **Bid rigging** | Firms collude on tender bids (e.g. rotate who wins) | Construction companies take turns winning government contracts |
| **Collective boycott** | Firms agree not to deal with a particular supplier/customer | |

> [!warning] Horizontal agreements are considered the **most harmful** because they directly replace competition with collusion. Many jurisdictions treat them as **per se illegal** (no defence).

### Vertical Agreements (Between Firms at Different Levels)

Agreements between firms at **different levels** of the supply chain:

| Practice | Description |
|---|---|
| **Resale price maintenance (RPM)** | Manufacturer sets the minimum price retailers must charge |
| **Exclusive dealing** | Retailer agrees not to stock competitors' products |
| **Tying / Bundling** | Sale of one product is conditional on buying another |
| **Selective distribution** | Manufacturer restricts which retailers may sell its products |

> [!note] Vertical agreements can sometimes have **pro-competitive** justifications (e.g. preventing free-riding on pre-sale services). Many are analysed under a **rule of reason** rather than being automatically illegal.

### Mergers

| Type | Description | Competition Concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal merger** | Merger between direct competitors | Reduces number of competitors → may create/strengthen market power |
| **Vertical merger** | Merger between a firm and its supplier or distributor | May foreclose rivals' access to inputs or customers |
| **Potential competition merger** | Merger with a firm that could have entered the market independently | Eliminates a potential future competitor |

> [!important] Competition authorities assess mergers by examining: market concentration, barriers to entry, efficiencies, and the likelihood of coordinated effects.

---

## Impact of Anti-competitive Practices

| Impact | Description |
|---|---|
| **Higher prices** | Less competition → firms can charge above competitive levels |
| **Lower output** | Firms restrict output to raise prices |
| **Reduced consumer surplus** | Consumers pay more and consume less |
| **Deadweight loss** | Allocative inefficiency (same as monopoly) |
| **Lower quality / less choice** | Less incentive to differentiate products |
| **Reduced innovation** | Without competitive pressure, firms may slack on R&D |
| **X-inefficiency** | Costs rise because there is no pressure to minimise costs |

> [!info] The total social cost of anti-competitive practices includes both the **deadweight loss** (allocative inefficiency) and **productive inefficiency** (X-inefficiency).

## Competition Ordinance in Hong Kong

### Background

The **Competition Ordinance (Cap. 619)** came into full effect on **14 December 2015**. It is enforced by the **Competition Commission**.

### Objectives

The Ordinance aims to:
- Prohibit anti-competitive conduct
- Promote and sustain competition in markets
- Enhance economic efficiency and consumer welfare

> [!note] The Ordinance does **not** prohibit monopoly itself — only the **abuse** of market power and certain anti-competitive agreements.

---

### First Conduct Rule (FCR) — Agreements Restricting Competition

> [!important] The **First Conduct Rule** prohibits agreements, concerted practices, or decisions by an association of undertakings that have the **object or effect** of preventing, restricting, or distorting competition in Hong Kong.

Applies to:
- **Horizontal agreements** between competitors (e.g. price fixing, market sharing, output restriction, bid rigging)
- **Vertical agreements** in certain circumstances (e.g. RPM)

Serious anti-competitive conduct (e.g. price fixing, bid rigging, market sharing, output restriction) is treated as:

> [!warning] **Serious anti-competitive conduct** — no defence or exemption is available for these practices.

---

### Second Conduct Rule (SCR) — Abuse of Market Power

> [!important] The **Second Conduct Rule** prohibits an undertaking with **substantial market power** from abusing that power by engaging in conduct that has the **object or effect** of preventing, restricting, or distorting competition.

Examples of abuse:
| Practice | Description |
|---|---|
| **Predatory pricing** | Setting prices below cost to drive out competitors |
| **Refusal to supply** | Denying access to an essential input |
| **Tying / Bundling** | Forcing customers to buy unrelated products |
| **Margin squeeze** | A vertically integrated firm charges such high wholesale prices that downstream competitors cannot compete |
| **Exclusive dealing** | Requiring customers to deal exclusively |

> [!important] **Market power** = the ability to act independently of competitive pressure. **Substantial market power** = significant market power (not necessarily monopoly). Having market power is **not** illegal — **abusing it** is.

---

### Exclusions and Exemptions

| Category | Details |
|---|---|
| **Statutory bodies** | Certain public bodies and statutory undertakings are excluded |
| **Agreements enhancing efficiency** | Exemption available if the agreement improves production/distribution or promotes technical/economic progress and benefits consumers |
| **Mergers** | The Competition Ordinance does **not** generally apply to mergers in most sectors (only applies to telecommunications and broadcasting mergers) |
| **Employees / Employment** | Employment terms are excluded |
| **Compliance with legal requirements** | Conduct required by law is not prohibited |
| **Agreements of lesser significance** | De minimis threshold — small agreements with negligible market impact may be excluded |

> [!info] The Competition Commission publishes **guidelines** on how it interprets and enforces the rules, including market share safe harbours for the First Conduct Rule (e.g. agreements between competitors whose combined market share is below 10% are generally unlikely to be pursued).

---

### Enforcement

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| **Competition Commission** | Investigates suspected contraventions, issues enforcement notices, brings proceedings before the Competition Tribunal |
| **Competition Tribunal** | Hears cases, imposes penalties (fines of up to 10% of Hong Kong turnover per year of infringement, for up to 3 years), director disqualification orders (up to 5 years) |
| **Leniency Policy** | The first cartel member to blow the whistle may receive immunity from or reduction in penalties |

### Private Actions

Parties who suffer loss or damage from an anti-competitive practice may:
- Seek **damages** or other remedies from the Competition Tribunal
- **Follow-on actions** — after the Tribunal has made a finding of infringement, affected parties can claim damages

## Comparison: Hong Kong vs Other Jurisdictions

| Feature | Hong Kong | EU | US |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horizontal agreements | FCR — prohibited | Article 101 — prohibited | Sherman Act §1 — prohibited |
| Abuse of dominance | SCR — prohibited | Article 102 — prohibited | Sherman Act §2 — monopolisation |
| Merger control | Only telecom/broadcasting | Mandatory notification | Mandatory notification |
| Penalty | Up to 10% of turnover (×3 years) | Up to 10% of worldwide turnover | Criminal (fines + imprisonment) |
| Private actions | Yes (follow-on) | Yes | Yes (treble damages) |

> [!example] **HKDSE exam tip**: You may be asked to identify whether a given scenario violates the FCR or SCR. Key distinction: FCR = **agreements** between two or more independent firms; SCR = **unilateral conduct** by a firm with substantial market power.
