# Efficiency, Equity and the Role of Government

## Efficiency

### Conditions for Efficiency

**Allocative efficiency** occurs when resources are allocated to their highest-valued uses — i.e. when **total social surplus is maximised**.

The condition is:

$$ MB = MC $$

At the competitive market equilibrium, $P = MB = MC$, so the sum of consumer and producer surplus is maximised. Any deviation reduces total surplus and creates a **deadweight loss**.

> [!info] **Pareto efficiency**: a situation where no one can be made better off without making someone worse off.

### Productive Efficiency

Producing at the **minimum point** of the average cost curve ($\min AC$). In perfect competition, this occurs in the long run.

| Type | Condition | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Allocative efficiency** | $P = MC$ | Right mix of goods is produced |
| **Productive efficiency** | $P = \min AC$ | Goods produced at lowest cost |

---

## Deviations from Efficiency

Any government intervention that moves the market away from equilibrium creates **deadweight loss**:

| Intervention | Effect | DWL |
|---|---|---|
| **Price ceiling** (below equilibrium) | Shortage; $Q < Q^*$ | Yes — lost trades from $Q$ to $Q^*$ |
| **Price floor** (above equilibrium) | Surplus; $Q < Q^*$ | Yes — lost trades from $Q^*$ to $Q$ |
| **Tax** | Shifts supply up; $Q < Q^*$ | Yes — tax wedge between $MB$ and $MC$ |
| **Subsidy** | Shifts supply down; $Q > Q^*$ | Yes — over-production beyond efficient level |
| **Quota** | Restricts output; $Q < Q^*$ | Yes — lost surplus on restricted units |

> [!warning] Deadweight loss is the **reduction in total social surplus** that results from a market distortion.

## Divergence Between Private and Social Costs/Benefits

**Externalities** occur when a transaction affects third parties not involved in the decision.

| Externality | Definition | Market Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Negative externality** | Social cost > Private cost (e.g. pollution) | **Over-production** — too much output |
| **Positive externality** | Social benefit > Private benefit (e.g. education) | **Under-production** — too little output |

### Graphical Analysis

For a negative externality:
- $MSC = MPC + MD$ (marginal social cost = marginal private cost + marginal damage)
- Market equilibrium at $MPC = MB$ → $Q_m > Q^*$
- DWL = triangle between $Q^*$ and $Q_m$

For a positive externality:
- $MSB = MPB + MEB$ (marginal social benefit = marginal private benefit + marginal external benefit)
- Market equilibrium at $MPB = MC$ → $Q_m < Q^*$
- DWL = triangle between $Q_m$ and $Q^*$

> [!example] **Negative externality**: A factory emits pollution. The private cost is labour and materials, but the social cost includes health damage to nearby residents. The market over-produces.

> [!example] **Positive externality**: A student gets vaccinated. The private benefit is personal protection, but society benefits from herd immunity. The market under-produces.

---

## Government Solutions to Externalities

| Problem | Solution | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Negative externality** | **Pigouvian tax** (equal to marginal external cost) | Tax shifts $MPC$ up to match $MSC$, internalising the externality |
| **Positive externality** | **Pigouvian subsidy** (equal to marginal external benefit) | Subsidy shifts $MPB$ down, increasing consumption to socially optimal level |
| **Both** | Regulation (bans, standards), tradable permits (cap-and-trade) | Direct quantity control |

> [!important] The Coase Theorem suggests that if property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are low, private bargaining can solve the externality problem **without government intervention**.

### Market Solutions
- **Coase Theorem**: assign property rights to either party — they will bargain to the efficient outcome
- **Mergers**: internalise the externality (e.g. an orchard and a bee-keeper merge)
- **Social norms**: voluntary agreements

## Equity

### Efficiency vs Equity in a Market Economy

A market economy achieves **efficiency** (the "pie" is as large as possible) but does **not** guarantee **equity** — how the pie is distributed. Some people may become very rich while others live in poverty.

> [!info] There is often a **trade-off** between equity and efficiency. Redistributive policies (progressive taxes, welfare) can distort incentives and reduce the size of the pie.

### Measuring Income Inequality

#### Lorenz Curve

The Lorenz curve plots the **cumulative share of income** against the **cumulative share of households** (from poorest to richest).

```
100% ┤
     │       /
     │      /
     │     / ← Lorenz curve
     │    /
     │   /
     │  /
  0% └─────────────
     0%   households   100%
```

- The **45° line** = perfect equality
- The further the Lorenz curve is from the 45° line, the **greater** the inequality

#### Gini Coefficient

$$ \text{Gini} = \frac{\text{Area between 45° line and Lorenz curve}}{\text{Area under 45° line}} $$

- $0$ = perfect equality
- $1$ = perfect inequality (one person receives all income)
- Higher Gini → higher inequality

> [!example] Hong Kong has a relatively high Gini coefficient (around 0.5), reflecting substantial income inequality.

### Sources of Income Inequality

| Source | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Human capital** | Differences in education, skills, experience |
| **Discrimination** | Gender, race, ethnicity, or other bias in hiring/wages |
| **Unequal ownership of capital** | Some individuals inherit wealth or own significant assets |
| **Personal abilities** | Natural talent, intelligence, health |
| **Luck / circumstance** | Family background, economic shocks |
| **Labour market structure** | Union power, minimum wage, segmentation |

## Policy Concerns

### Equalising Income vs Equalising Opportunities

| Approach | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Equalising income** | Progressive income tax, welfare transfers, unemployment benefits |
| **Equalising opportunities** | Free education, vocational training, anti-discrimination laws, healthcare access |

> [!important] Equalising opportunities aims to create a **level playing field** without directly redistributing income. It is generally less distortionary than redistribution.

### Disincentive Effects

Policies that reduce inequality can also reduce work/effort:

| Policy | Potential Disincentive |
|---|---|
| Progressive income tax | High earners may work less or avoid tax |
| Welfare benefits | May reduce incentive to seek employment (poverty / unemployment trap) |
| Minimum wage | May reduce hiring of low-skilled workers |

### Trade-off Between Equity and Efficiency

> [!warning] Most redistributive policies involve an **equity-efficiency trade-off**: reducing inequality comes at the cost of lower total output. The art of policy design is minimising this trade-off.

| View | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Prioritise efficiency** | A larger pie benefits everyone eventually (trickle-down) |
| **Prioritise equity** | Inequality leads to social unrest, lower human capital formation, and inefficient under-consumption by the poor |
