# Extension of Trade Theory

## Comparative Costs and Gains from Trade with PPF

The **Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)** illustrates the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce given its resources and technology.

### Autarky (No Trade)

Under autarky, a country's consumption is **limited to its PPF**. The slope of the PPF at any point represents the **marginal rate of transformation** (MRT), which equals the **opportunity cost** of producing one good in terms of the other.

$$MRT = \frac{MC_x}{MC_y} = \text{Opportunity cost of X in terms of Y}$$

> [!info] In autarky, a country's consumption possibilities are bounded by its production possibilities. **Trade breaks this constraint.**

### With Trade

When a country specialises according to its **comparative advantage** and trades at international prices, it can consume **beyond** its PPF.

> [!example] **PPF Analysis of Comparative Advantage**
> 
> **Country A**: PPF is linear — can produce 100 food or 200 cloth
> - Opportunity cost of 1 food = 2 cloth
> - Opportunity cost of 1 cloth = 0.5 food
> 
> **Country B**: PPF is linear — can produce 80 food or 80 cloth
> - Opportunity cost of 1 food = 1 cloth
> - Opportunity cost of 1 cloth = 1 food
> 
> **Comparative advantage**:
> - Country A has **comparative advantage in cloth** (lower opportunity cost: 0.5 < 1)
> - Country B has **comparative advantage in food** (lower opportunity cost: 1 < 2)
> 
> **Terms of trade**: Suppose 1 food = 1.5 cloth (between both countries' domestic ratios)
> - Country A specialises in cloth: produces 200 cloth, trades some for food
> - Country B specialises in food: produces 80 food, trades some for cloth
> - Both countries end up **outside their PPFs** — pure gains from trade

### Conditions for Mutually Beneficial Trade

1. **Different opportunity cost ratios** between countries (otherwise no comparative advantage)
2. **Terms of trade** lie strictly between the two countries' domestic opportunity cost ratios
3. Countries specialise in their comparative advantage goods

### Complete vs Incomplete Specialisation

| Scenario | Outcome |
|----------|---------|
| **Increasing opportunity costs** (concave PPF) | Likely **incomplete** specialisation — each country continues producing some of both goods |
| **Constant opportunity costs** (linear PPF) | Likely **complete** specialisation — each country produces only its comparative advantage good |

## Comparative Advantage and Globalisation

### The Role of Comparative Advantage in Globalisation

> [!important] Comparative advantage is the **intellectual foundation** of globalisation. The principle shows that even countries with no absolute advantage gain from trade, making trade mutually beneficial for all participants.

### Factors That Shape Comparative Advantage

| Factor | How It Affects Comparative Advantage |
|--------|--------------------------------------|
| **Factor endowments** (Heckscher-Ohlin model) | Countries export goods that intensively use their abundant factors |
| **Technology** | Technological leaders have comparative advantage in high-tech goods |
| **Labour skills** | Skilled labour abundant countries in skill-intensive goods |
| **Climate and geography** | Agricultural comparative advantage from natural conditions |
| **Scale economies** | Specialisation allows cost reduction through large-scale production |

### Globalisation and the Expansion of Trade

- **Reduced trade barriers** (WTO rounds, regional trade agreements)
- **Transport and communication cost declines**
- **Global supply chains** (fragmentation of production across borders)
- **Foreign direct investment** and multinational corporations

> [!warning] While comparative advantage predicts net gains from trade, globalisation also creates **distributional effects** — workers in import-competing industries may lose, requiring adjustment assistance and retraining.

### Criticisms and Limitations of Comparative Advantage

| Limitation | Explanation |
|------------|-------------|
| **Static analysis** | Does not account for dynamic changes in productivity |
| **Factor mobility** | Assumes factors can move freely between industries |
| **Full employment** | Assumes resources are fully employed |
| **No externalities** | Ignores environmental and social costs |

> [!info] Despite these limitations, comparative advantage remains a **powerful and robust** tool for understanding the gains from trade and the basis for globalisation.
