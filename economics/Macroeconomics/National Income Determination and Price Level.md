# National Income Determination and Price Level

## Aggregate Demand (AD)

**Aggregate Demand** is the total quantity of goods and services that all economic agents (households, firms, government, foreign buyers) are willing to buy at different price levels, **ceteris paribus**.

### Why the AD Curve is Downward Sloping

$$AD = C + I + G + NX$$

Three effects explain the inverse relationship between price level ($P$) and real GDP ($Y$):

1. **Wealth (Real Balances) Effect**: Lower $P \to$ higher real value of money holdings $\to$ higher $C$
2. **Interest Rate Effect**: Lower $P \to$ lower money demand $\to$ lower $i \to$ higher $I$ and $C$
3. **Exchange Rate Effect**: Lower $P \to$ lower $i \to$ currency depreciation $\to$ higher $NX$

> [!info] The AD curve is NOT the same as a microeconomic demand curve. The AD curve shows the relationship between the **overall price level** and total output, not the price of a single good.

### Determinants of AD (Shift Factors)

| Component | Factors that Increase AD |
|-----------|-------------------------|
| **Consumption ($C$)** | Higher disposable income, lower taxes, higher consumer confidence, lower interest rates, wealth increase |
| **Investment ($I$)** | Lower interest rates, optimistic business expectations, technological progress, tax incentives |
| **Government ($G$)** | Increased infrastructure spending, higher public services, defence spending |
| **Net Exports ($NX$)** | Weaker currency, higher foreign income, lower trade barriers |

## Aggregate Supply (AS)

### Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)

The SRAS curve slopes **upward** because:

- **Sticky wages**: Nominal wages adjust slowly. If $P$ rises, real wages fall $\to$ firms hire more $\to$ output rises
- **Sticky prices**: Some firms lag in adjusting prices $\to$ relative price changes induce output changes
- **Imperfect information**: Producers may mistake general price rises for increased demand for their own goods

### Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)

The LRAS curve is **vertical** at the full-employment level of output ($Y_f$). In the long run:

- All prices and wages are fully **flexible**
- Output is determined by **real factors** (labour, capital, technology), not the price level
- Economy is at **full employment** / potential output

### Factors Affecting SRAS and LRAS

| Factor | Effect on AS |
|--------|-------------|
| **Resource prices** (oil, raw materials) | Increase in input prices $\to$ SRAS shifts left |
| **Nominal wages** | Higher wages $\to$ SRAS shifts left |
| **Technology** | Improvement $\to$ both SRAS and LRAS shift right |
| **Labour force** | Growth $\to$ both SRAS and LRAS shift right |
| **Capital stock** | Increase $\to$ both SRAS and LRAS shift right |
| **Expectations of future prices** | Higher expected $P \to$ SRAS shifts left |

## The AS-AD Model

### Short-Run Equilibrium

Equilibrium output ($Y_e$) and price level ($P_e$) determined by intersection of AD and SRAS:

$$Y_e = Y \quad \text{where} \quad AD = SRAS$$

### Long-Run Equilibrium

In long-run equilibrium:
$$Y_e = Y_f \quad \text{and} \quad AD = SRAS = LRAS$$

> [!important] In the long run, the economy **self-corrects**. Any deviation from $Y_f$ triggers wage/price adjustments that return the economy to potential output.

## Changes in Equilibrium

### Shift in AD (Demand Shocks)

| Scenario | Short Run | Long Run (Self-Correction) |
|----------|-----------|---------------------------|
| **AD $\uparrow$** (e.g., expansionary fiscal policy) | $Y \uparrow$, $P \uparrow$ | Wages rise $\to$ SRAS $\leftarrow$ $\to$ $Y$ returns to $Y_f$, $P$ higher |
| **AD $\downarrow$** (e.g., recession abroad) | $Y \downarrow$, $P \downarrow$ | Wages fall $\to$ SRAS $\rightarrow$ $\to$ $Y$ returns to $Y_f$, $P$ lower |

### Shift in AS (Supply Shocks)

| Scenario | Effect |
|----------|--------|
| **Negative supply shock** (e.g., oil price spike) | SRAS $\leftarrow$ $\to$ $Y \downarrow$, $P \uparrow$ (**stagflation**) |
| **Positive supply shock** (e.g., tech breakthrough) | SRAS $\rightarrow$ $\to$ $Y \uparrow$, $P \downarrow$ |

> [!warning] **Stagflation** is a particularly challenging scenario: rising prices and falling output mean policymakers face a painful trade-off.

### Adjustment Mechanism from Short Run to Long Run

> [!example] **Deflationary (Recessionary) Gap**
> If $Y_e < Y_f$, the economy is in a recessionary gap. High unemployment puts downward pressure on wages. SRAS shifts right, $Y$ rises and $P$ falls, until $Y = Y_f$.

> [!example] **Inflationary Gap**
> If $Y_e > Y_f$, the economy is overheating. Low unemployment pushes wages up. SRAS shifts left, $Y$ falls and $P$ rises further, until $Y = Y_f$.

## Employment and Output

The relationship between employment and output is captured by the **production function**:

$$Y = A \cdot F(K, L)$$

- $Y$ = real output
- $A$ = total factor productivity
- $K$ = capital stock
- $L$ = labour input

As employment rises (towards full employment), output rises. Beyond full employment ($Y > Y_f$), the economy is operating beyond capacity, which is unsustainable.
